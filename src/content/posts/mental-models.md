> Over the last two years, I’ve made my fair share of bad decisions — both personally and professionally.
> 
> Some of them were impulsive, some were avoidable, and some I didn’t even realize were mistakes until much later.
> 
> That stretch of time forced me to slow down and start paying closer attention to how I think — not just what I think.
> 
> I began writing things down, questioning my assumptions, and looking for clearer ways to process decisions.
> 
> Some of these I picked up from reading. Others came from experience — often after getting something wrong.

### **First Principles thinking**

One mental model that really shifted how I handle problems is **First Principles thinking**. For a long time, I found myself stuck in a pattern of just copying what others did or blindly following conventional wisdom without stopping to ask if it really made sense for me. It felt like I was running on autopilot, repeating what seemed “normal” without questioning why.

I remember reading about Elon Musk and how he approached building rockets. Instead of accepting the insanely high costs as a given, he broke everything down to raw materials and the basic physics behind it. That idea stuck with me — stripping away all the assumptions to get to the core truths. That’s what First Principles is about.

So I started trying it myself. Whenever I faced a big decision or a complicated problem, I’d ask: _What am I actually trying to achieve? What do I know for sure? And which assumptions am I making that I could test or just ignore?_ It helped me focus on what really mattered instead of getting lost in noise or trying to follow shortcuts that didn’t fit.

For example, during my PhD experiments, I often found myself running on autopilot — designing experiments or following protocols without fully analyzing the underlying assumptions I was making. Sometimes, I’d realize later that I had accepted certain “givens” that didn’t really apply to my specific research question.

By deliberately challenging those hidden assumptions — like questioning if the baseline model was really appropriate or if the evaluation metrics truly reflected the goals — I could rethink my approach from scratch. This led me to simpler, clearer experimental designs that were better aligned with what I wanted to learn, instead of just repeating what I’d seen others do.

Before, without this way of thinking, I’d get caught up in habits or advice that didn’t really suit my situation. I wasted time and energy on things that felt right because others did them, but didn’t actually work for me.

In both my research and personal projects, breaking problems down into their fundamental parts has helped me find clearer, simpler paths forward. It’s not always easy or fast, but it’s freeing. Instead of copying the crowd, I’m building my own way — step by step, from the ground up.

### **Second-Order Thinking**

Another mental model that’s helped me think more clearly is **Second-Order Thinking**. Too often, we focus on the immediate outcome — the quick win or the obvious effect — without stopping to consider what might come after. This blind spot can lead to surprises, frustrations, or unintended problems down the road. Second-Order Thinking is about going beyond the first consequence and asking yourself, _“What happens after that? And after that?”_ It’s like playing chess: you don’t just move the piece in front of you, you try to anticipate how the game will unfold several moves ahead.

I try to use this way of thinking whenever I’m making decisions that seem simple but might have ripple effects. It’s not about overcomplicating things or getting stuck in analysis paralysis, but about being aware that every choice can trigger a chain of outcomes — some helpful, some harmful. Without this mindset, it’s easy to chase quick fixes that create bigger problems later. We get caught in cycles where the solution today becomes the headache tomorrow.

For example, in my coding work, I often prioritized delivering results quickly. I’d write fast, messy code to test ideas or meet deadlines, thinking speed was the most important thing. At first, it worked — I got results fast. But because the code wasn’t maintainable, when bugs inevitably appeared, I ended up spending weeks untangling and fixing issues that could have been avoided with a bit more foresight. That’s the classic second-order effect: saving time upfront only to lose much more later. If I’d paused to consider how those quick fixes might impact the long-term health of the code, I could have built cleaner solutions from the start, saving myself a lot of stress and extra work.

In business, decisions often have ripple effects that aren’t obvious at first. For example, cutting costs might improve short-term profits, but it can also reduce quality or slow down growth over time. Launching a new product quickly might grab attention, but if the product isn’t ready, it can hurt the brand and customer trust in the long run. These are classic examples of second-order consequences — the results of our actions echoing beyond the immediate impact.

If you want to dive deeper into how second-order consequences play out in real life, I wrote a longer piece about it [here](/writing/delayed-gratification/).

### **Inversion**

Some problems are hard to solve not because we don’t know what to do — but because we’re asking the question the wrong way. That’s where **Inversion** comes in. Instead of asking _“How can I succeed?”_, you flip the question: _“How could this completely fail?”_ And suddenly, things that felt blurry become obvious.

Inversion is about thinking in reverse — not just chasing the ideal outcome, but deliberately identifying what to avoid. It’s surprisingly effective at bringing clarity, especially when you’re overwhelmed by too many options, too much pressure, or not enough time. We usually default to asking “what should I do next?” But sometimes, the more useful question is: _“What would absolutely sabotage this?”_

One situation where this helped me was during the rebuttal period for a paper submission. As always, the time window was tight — just a week — and the initial instinct was to fix everything: run more experiments, rewrite parts of the method section, create additional ablations, polish the tone. It felt overwhelming. But then I flipped the question: _“What would make this rebuttal fail?”_ The answers came fast — trying to treat all reviewer requests equally, wasting time on low-impact experiments, or focusing on polishing details while ignoring the most critical concerns.

That shift in mindset helped me prioritize what actually mattered. I didn’t need to run 10 more experiments — I needed to clearly address the core issues, communicate respectfully, and show we understood the reviewers’ points. Instead of chasing every possible improvement, I focused on avoiding the obvious failure modes.

In situations where several paths seem valid and the pressure to choose quickly is high, this mindset helps cut through the noise and focus on what actually matters.

Instead of asking _“What’s the best move?”_, ask _“Which path has the highest risk of going wrong?”_ Inversion isn’t about being negative — it’s about removing avoidable mistakes so what’s left can actually work.

### **Opportunity Cost**

One mental model that often comes up when decisions pile up or commitments start to feel overwhelming is **Opportunity Cost**. Though it sounds like a dry economics term, it’s surprisingly practical — and eye-opening once you really start noticing it.

At its core, opportunity cost is the idea that _every choice comes at the cost of something else you didn’t choose_. Every time you say yes to one thing — a meeting, a side project, a task — you’re saying no to something else, whether you realize it or not.

The cost isn’t just time or energy. Sometimes it’s focus. Or progress. Or rest.

For a long time, I’d take on more than I could realistically handle — out of curiosity, out of guilt, or out of not wanting to miss out (FOMO). But I rarely paused to ask: _“What am I trading away by doing this?”_ Once that question became part of how I thought, things started to shift.

Imagine a startup founder juggling dozens of opportunities—pitching to investors, chasing every new feature request, exploring multiple partnerships—all at once. On paper, it looks like momentum and hustle. But in reality, none of those efforts get the focus they deserve, and progress stalls. When the founder steps back and applies the idea of opportunity cost, it becomes clear that saying yes to everything means saying no to building a strong product and a clear vision. That realization can be tough, but it forces a much-needed focus on what truly moves the needle.

The power of this model lies in forcing real clarity. It’s not just about asking, “Is this worth doing?” but digging deeper to ask, “Is this worth doing _instead of_ everything else I could be focusing on?” Saying yes to one thing means you’re giving your attention, energy, and time away from other possibilities — even if it feels like you’re being productive across many fronts. This question helps zoom out and see where commitments might be spreading you too thin or pulling you in conflicting directions.

Opportunity cost often shows up in the smallest choices — like spending an hour perfecting a graphic when writing that key section would move a project forward more meaningfully. Other times, it’s bigger — taking on a new project that seems important but quietly steals focus and momentum from the work that truly matters.

Remembering that “doing one thing always means not doing another” has helped many be more intentional and, perhaps more importantly, find peace in what they choose _not_ to pursue. It’s a reminder that focus isn’t just about adding more, but about choosing carefully where to direct your limited energy.

### **Leverage**

One mental model that often changes how you approach _problems_ and _effort_ is **Leverage**. At its core, leverage is about finding points where a small input can create a disproportionately large output. It’s the idea of working smarter, not just harder — but it’s more than just a cliché; it’s a practical mindset that can transform how you spend your time and energy.

Leverage comes in many forms: using tools to automate repetitive tasks, focusing on the few key experiments that unlock deeper insights, or choosing collaborations that amplify your impact. The challenge — and opportunity — is spotting where leverage exists in the chaos of daily work.

Take content creation for example. Influencers and companies often spend countless hours producing content, but the real magic happens when a single piece of content goes viral and attracts millions of views, followers, or customers. That one video, post, or article can bring in more attention and business than dozens of smaller efforts combined. This is leverage in action — creating something once that keeps working to bring in clients and opportunities over time without needing to be recreated constantly.

Similarly, SaaS businesses provide a great illustration of leverage. Writing code is hard work upfront, but when done well, that code can serve millions of users repeatedly with minimal additional effort. The product scales because the initial investment leverages technology to multiply impact. Of course, some maintenance is always necessary, but the leverage lies in building something once that keeps delivering value over and over.

Without leverage, it’s easy to fall into the trap of grinding through endless tasks without moving the needle. But once you identify where a little extra effort yields exponential returns, you start to shift your entire approach. It helps you prioritize what to invest in and, equally important, what to avoid.

Leverage doesn’t mean doing less; it means doing what truly counts by channeling your efforts for maximum effect.

### **Circle of Competence**

Another simple but powerful mental model for staying focused and avoiding overreach is the **Circle of Competence**. It’s a simple but powerful idea: _know clearly what you understand well—and just as importantly, know what you don’t_. Staying inside your circle means focusing your efforts on areas where you have expertise, experience, or a solid grasp. It’s about avoiding the trap of overconfidence or stretching yourself too thin by chasing things outside your reach.

This model isn’t about staying comfortable or avoiding challenges altogether. Instead, it’s about making smarter choices by recognizing your limits and playing to your strengths. When you operate inside your circle, decisions become clearer, and risks become more manageable.

In many fields, it’s tempting to jump on every new trend or tool that promises quick results. However, stepping back to ask, “Do we truly understand this well enough to apply it effectively?” can prevent wasted effort and frustration. It’s often more effective to deepen expertise in familiar areas than to spread resources too thin chasing unfamiliar territory, especially in research work.

This mindset is equally useful in everyday life—whether it’s learning a new skill, investing time in relationships, or making career moves. Recognizing what’s in your circle helps you focus on what you can control and improve, rather than guessing or overreaching.

Understanding where your true expertise lies helps focus effort, avoid costly mistakes, and build confidence in decision-making.

At the end of the day, mental models aren’t just abstract ideas — they’re practical tools that help us think clearer, decide smarter, and live with more intention. The more you practice using them, the more natural they become. Here’s to reasoning better, one model at a time.
